Mapping Digital Infrastructure in London and Southeast England

The question

How does London connect to international digital networks despite being an inland city? I wanted to explore the relationship between the electricity and telecommunications infrastructure in London and the submarine cable landing points along the southeast coast of England. I was particularly interested in how coastal locations such as Brighton, Newhaven, and Pevensey Bay contribute to a wider regional network that supports digital connectivity in London.

The data

I used three main sources. OpenStreetMap (OSM), accessed on 2 November 2026, is maintained by the OpenStreetMap community and distributed under the Open Database License (ODbL). Its data can be reused with attribution and under the licence's share-alike requirements. I exported the OSM power data as GeoJSON and published it as a hosted feature layer in ArcGIS Online.

The second source was TeleGeography's Submarine Cable Map, accessed in November 2026. It provided information about cable routes, landing points, owners, and service dates. Map references and screenshots may be reused under CC BY-SA 4.0 with attribution, but the underlying database is restricted to subscribers. I therefore recorded the landing points manually rather than downloading its database.

The third source was Data Center Map, accessed on 2 November 2026 and operated by Data Center Map ApS. Its Terms of Use restrict automated data collection and unauthorized copying or republication. I used manual browsing and linked to the original website instead of reproducing its database.

The method

I used Overpass Turbo to query two groups of OSM objects. The telecommunications query was: 

[out:json][timeout:60];
nwr["telecom"]({{bbox}});
out center;

The power query was: 

[out:json][timeout:120];
(
  nwr["power"="substation"]({{bbox}});
  nwr["power"="tower"]({{bbox}});
);
out center;

The first query returned 620 telecommunications-related objects, including 80 nodes, 532 ways, and 8 relations. The second returned 16,269 power infrastructure objects: 8,806 substations and 7,463 power towers. Both queries used the visible map extent covering London and parts of southeast England, including the coastal area around Brighton. I used the overpass-api.de server. The telecom response recorded an OSM data timestamp of 2 November 2026, 12:20:27 UTC.

The evidence

The map combines OSM power infrastructure with four manually recorded submarine cable landing points: Brighton, Newhaven, Pevensey Bay, and Portsmouth. These points represent the British ends of Crosslake Fibre, Q&E South, Circe South, and Portsmouth–Ryde 10 respectively. The OSM layer was exported as GeoJSON, while the landing points were entered separately using coordinates read from TeleGeography.

The evidence

What I found

The map shows a dense distribution of mapped electricity infrastructure across London and much of southeast England. The concentration of points is particularly visible around London and along several major corridors extending towards the south coast. This suggests that digital infrastructure should not be understood only at the scale of an individual city.

An important finding is that London, despite being an inland city, is connected to international submarine cable networks through coastal landing points in the surrounding region. Brighton, Newhaven, and Pevensey Bay provide examples of locations where international submarine cables reach the British coast. The map brings these coastal nodes together with the wider distribution of inland infrastructure, suggesting a degree of regional continuity between coastal connectivity and the urban networks that support London. In this sense, London's digital connectivity extends beyond its administrative boundaries and depends on infrastructure distributed across different places and geographical scales.

Data Center Map's London page displayed 226 facilities from 162 operators in the browser tab, while the page body stated that it listed 273 data center locations. This difference also highlights how commercial databases may use different counting units.

What this does not show

The map does not show the actual routes of underground telecommunications cables connecting the coastal landing points to London. The spatial relationship between the landing points and inland infrastructure suggests regional connectivity, but it does not prove the exact route or operator of those connections.

The relatively small number of mapped power points around Portsmouth may partly reflect the western boundary of my original Overpass query extent, which did not fully cover the area around the Portsmouth landing point. This should not be interpreted as evidence that Portsmouth has little electricity infrastructure. In addition, OSM records depend on what contributors can identify and map. Underground infrastructure and facilities that cannot be observed directly may be underrepresented.

I am using mapped digital infrastructure to represent the regional networks supporting London's connectivity, and it might fail because OpenStreetMap contributors record infrastructure from observable sources and local knowledge, so underground connections and facilities that cannot be identified from public observation may remain unmapped.
