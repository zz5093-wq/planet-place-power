From Maps to a StoryMap: Green Space and Recorded Crime in London

The question

London's greenery and recorded-crime distributions cannot yet be compared reliably across their different mapping units.

This week's StoryMap asks whether a greener London also means a safer London. I use the pattern of vegetation in selected places and the distribution of recorded crime by ward to explore a possible relationship. However, the two datasets do not yet measure the same geographic units.

The data

I reviewed my Week 02–08 journal entries to identify the spatial unit behind each map, its publication conditions, and how far I traced its data to the original source. The table includes all seven weeks, including maps I decided not to use in this StoryMap.

For the StoryMap, I also introduced a new dataset: Police.uk street-level crime records from the Metropolitan Police and City of London Police, covering October 2025 to September 2026. I excluded anti-social behaviour records and used anonymised crime coordinates to allocate records to May 2026 ward boundaries. These are approximate ward-level crime counts, not official ward totals or population-adjusted crime rates.

The method

I selected two maps for the first StoryMap draft: my earlier NDVI map and a newly created London ward crime map. I left out the other maps because they either focus on different issues or do not directly contribute to the relationship I want to examine.

I placed the NDVI map first to introduce differences in urban greenery, followed by the crime map to examine another dimension of London's urban environment. This order follows the research question rather than the chronology of my assignments.

An important change occurs between the maps: the NDVI analysis uses 30 m raster pixels in selected locations, while the crime map uses administrative ward polygons across Greater London. These are different spatial units, so the maps cannot yet be compared directly to establish a statistical relationship.

The evidence

View my published StoryMap: Does a Greener London Mean a Safer London? (A3 draft)

The StoryMap combines my earlier vegetation analysis with newly mapped crime data. It is an initial exploration rather than the final analysis.

What I found

Putting these maps together revealed a limitation that was not obvious when looking at either map alone. The NDVI map shows vegetation differences across selected sites, while the crime map shows police-recorded offences across administrative wards. Their combination raises a question about urban greenery and safety, but also demonstrates why choosing consistent mapping units is essential before comparing spatial patterns.

What this does not show

The most important missing map is a ward-level map of green-cover percentages. I also need population data to calculate crime rates rather than compare raw crime counts. These additional datasets would allow me to examine greenery and recorded crime using more consistent spatial units.

Until then, I cannot conclude whether greener wards tend to experience higher or lower recorded crime rates. Even if a relationship appears, it would not establish causation. Other factors, such as land use, commuting, socioeconomic conditions, and differences in crime reporting, may also shape the patterns.

The environmental and crime datasets also cover different time periods. Police.uk locations are anonymised, so the ward-level counts derived from them may contain spatial allocation errors.
