Mapping UK Exports, 2024

The question

Where did the United Kingdom export its products and services in 2024, and what can a destination-level map tell us about the geography of trade? I wanted to compare export values across countries and connect this global pattern to the UK's services economy. I was particularly interested in whether the map revealed a concentration of exports in nearby European countries, as well as the importance of more distant destinations such as China.

The data

I used the Atlas of Economic Complexity's UK export data for 2024 and Natural Earth's Admin 0 – Countries boundary dataset at 1:110m scale. The CSV contains destination names, gross export values, and export shares. I joined the destination data to country polygons by matching the CSV's Name field with Natural Earth's ADMIN field. I also prepared a point layer showing the City of London, Canary Wharf, and Heathrow Airport as illustrative locations associated with the UK's services economy and international connectivity.

The method

I created a joined view in ArcGIS Online and styled Gross_Export using Counts and Amounts (Color) to make a choropleth map. I then added the three illustrative locations as a point layer. To check the join, I examined boundary records without a matched export value and separately compared CSV destination names against the boundary names. These checks helped identify potential omissions caused by differences between the datasets.

The evidence

What I found

London has a highly developed services economy, but the export map suggests that UK exports are concentrated in nearby destinations, particularly in Europe, while China also appears as an important destination. This pattern may reflect the UK's strong economic ties with European markets and its connections with major global trading partners. It could also be influenced by policy and institutional factors, including visa requirements, customs procedures, and other barriers to cross-border exchange. However, some of the apparent concentration may result from missing data or unmatched country names. The map reveals the geographical pattern of reported exports, but further research would be needed to determine how much is explained by economic relationships, policy differences, or data limitations.

The point layer adds a physical perspective to this pattern. The City of London and Canary Wharf represent important financial and business districts, while Heathrow illustrates international transport connectivity. These locations help connect the UK's services economy with the infrastructure and business activities that support international exchange. However, the points are illustrative rather than verified origins of the export values shown on the map.

What this does not show

The join depends on matching country names across two datasets. In the joined boundary table, 13 boundary records had no matched export value. In the opposite direction, 59 CSV records did not have an exact name match in the boundary dataset. These figures come from different directions of checking and should not be added together. They also do not mean that all these places had no trade, since differences in naming conventions can prevent records from matching.

The choropleth shows destination-level export values, not the routes, firms, or specific places responsible for trade. It cannot establish that visa requirements, customs procedures, or other UK policies caused the observed geographical pattern. The CSV also does not itself clearly specify the currency unit, so this should be verified against the source metadata before interpreting the values as USD.
