Week 07 Journal: Mapping UK Exports, 2024

1. Expectation

I used the Atlas of Economic Complexity to examine the United Kingdom’s export destinations in 2024. In the product view, services appeared as the largest broad category, with an export value of approximately $235 billion and a share of 21.35%. This is a broad category rather than a specific physical product, which changes how I interpret the idea of locating an export. Many services are delivered digitally or through professional networks rather than shipped from a factory or port. The results show that the UK’s export economy cannot be understood only through physical commodities and their production locations.

2. Data and method

I used the Atlas of Economic Complexity’s 2024 UK export-destination data and Natural Earth Admin 0 – Countries (1:110m) as the boundary layer. In ArcGIS Online, I created a left join using the ADMIN field in the boundary layer and the Name field in the export table. I then styled the joined layer using Counts and Amounts (Color), based on the export-value field. Finally, I added a point layer showing the City of London, Canary Wharf, and Heathrow Airport as illustrative locations associated with financial, business, travel, and international connectivity services.

3. The join

The join used exact name matching rather than geographic similarity. Of the 177 records in the boundary layer, 13 had no matched export value. These blank values should not automatically be interpreted as zero exports: they may result from differences between country names in the two datasets. A left join retains boundary records even when no matching table row is found. The reverse direction must also be checked separately to identify export-table rows that did not match any polygon. The two fields used were ADMIN (boundary layer) and Name (export table).

4. The physical link

The three selected points provide examples of locations associated with services and international connections. The City of London and Canary Wharf are business and financial districts, while Heathrow Airport is an international aviation hub. These locations help illustrate that service exports may depend on office networks, professional expertise, and transport connections rather than a single extraction site or factory. However, the points do not show measured export values and do not prove that these specific locations generated a particular share of UK exports.

5. Limits

The choropleth displays export values by destination, but shading an entire country can make a trade total appear to be a property of every location within its borders. The map does not show exactly where value was produced, who benefited, or how each service was delivered. The 1:110m boundary data generalises geography, and the name-based join leaves 13 boundary records without matched export values. The three points are selective examples rather than a complete representation of the UK service economy. Therefore, the map should not be used to infer local economic activity directly from national trade totals.
